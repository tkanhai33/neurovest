      Dynamic Factor Allocation via Momentum-Based
                    Regime Switching

                                              Abstract

          We present a systematic framework for dynamic allocation across equity fac-
       tors (Value, Size, Momentum, Quality, Growth) using a momentum-based regime
       switching model. Using a z-score normalization approach with only two hyper-
       parameters, we identify Bull and Bear regimes for each factor through normalized
       trend signals. Our regime identification proves statistically significant across all fac-
       tors, with Size and Growth showing strong significance (p < 0.01). Cross-sectional
       validation confirms that Bull regimes exhibit systematic positive forward returns
       (t = 1.98, p = 0.047), while Bear regimes show no significant pattern, support-
       ing asymmetric treatment in portfolio construction. The factor timing strategy
       achieves a Sharpe ratio of 0.66 compared to 0.59 for an equal-weighted benchmark,
       with annualized returns of 13.0% versus 11.3% over the 1998-2025 period. The
       strategy demonstrates consistent outperformance, with a 78.6% calendar year hit
       rate, while maintaining manageable turnover of approximately 3x annually. We
       validate practical implementability through ETF-based backtesting, finding only
       modest performance degradation from tracking error and expense ratios. Our re-
       sults support the hypothesis that factor momentum exhibits sufficient persistence
       to be captured by simple trend-following signals.

1    Introduction
Equity factors such as Value, Size, and Momentum have historically provided long-term
risk premia. However, these factors exhibit cyclicality, with periods of underperformance
or outperformance that can persist for months to years.

This research explores a “Factor Momentum” approach, positing that factor momentum
exhibits persistence. We propose a simple but robust signal generation methodology
based on trend indicators to identify the prevailing regime for each factor. These regime
signals are then translated into portfolio views within a Black-Litterman optimization
process to dynamically tilt the portfolio towards favorable factors.


2    Literature Review
The intellectual origins of factor investing trace to the Capital Asset Pricing Model
(CAPM) of Sharpe [1964] and Lintner [1965], which established market beta as the sole
systematic risk factor explaining expected returns. However, subsequent empirical work
documented anomalies inconsistent with single-factor pricing. Basu [1977] identified the
value effect, showing that low price-to-earnings stocks outperformed, while Banz [1981]
documented the size premium favoring small-capitalization equities.

The foundational work by Fama and French [1993] synthesized these anomalies into a
coherent three-factor model extending CAPM with size (SMB) and value (HML) factors,
establishing the framework for modern factor investing. This was later expanded to five
factors incorporating profitability (RMW) and investment (CMA) in Fama and French
[2015], reflecting the influence of Novy-Marx [2013] on the role of profitability in cross-
sectional returns. Carhart [1997] added momentum (WML) as a fourth factor, building
on the seminal work of Jegadeesh and Titman [1993] who documented that past winners
continue to outperform past losers over 3-12 month horizons.

The quality factor, though less formally defined in early academic literature, gained
prominence through practitioner research. Asness et al. [2019] provide a comprehen-
sive definition linking quality to profitability, growth, safety, and payout characteris-
tics, demonstrating that high-quality stocks command a premium across global markets.
Frazzini and Pedersen [2014] document the “betting against beta” phenomenon, show-
ing that low-beta stocks deliver higher risk-adjusted returns than predicted by CAPM,
further enriching the factor zoo.

Evidence suggests that factor returns exhibit momentum patterns analogous to individ-
ual securities. Arnott et al. [2018] document that factors themselves display momentum
characteristics, with recent outperformers tending to continue outperforming over inter-


                                            2
mediate horizons of 1-12 months. This “factor momentum” effect is distinct from the
stock-level momentum factor and suggests that tactical allocation across factors may en-
hance returns. Ehsani and Linnainmaa [2022] extend this analysis, demonstrating that
factor momentum subsumes much of the stock-level momentum premium and persists
across international markets.

Asness et al. [2013] examine value and momentum interactions across asset classes, finding
that factor momentum persists globally and that value and momentum exhibit negative
correlation, providing diversification benefits when combined. Their work on “value ev-
erywhere” establishes that the same behavioral and risk-based mechanisms driving factor
premia in equities apply to fixed income, currencies, and commodities.

The predictability of factor returns has been explored through various conditioning vari-
ables. Gupta and Kelly [2019] demonstrate that factor timing strategies based on momen-
tum signals, valuation spreads, and macroeconomic indicators can enhance risk-adjusted
returns, though with significant implementation challenges. They find that cross-sectional
predictors (e.g., factor valuations) and time-series predictors (e.g., momentum) offer com-
plementary information. Avramov and Chordia [2006] show that macroeconomic vari-
ables such as credit spreads and term structure slopes predict factor returns, particularly
during recessions. Cederburg et al. [2023] provide a comprehensive out-of-sample evalu-
ation of factor timing strategies, finding modest but economically meaningful improve-
ments in Sharpe ratios when using simple trend-following rules.

Importantly, Haddad et al. [2020] argue that factor timing opportunities arise from time-
varying risk aversion rather than mispricing, with factors offering higher expected returns
when their valuations are stretched. This risk-based interpretation suggests that factor
timing strategies capture compensation for bearing time-varying systematic risk rather
than exploiting behavioral inefficiencies.


2.1    Contribution
Our work differs from existing literature in several key respects. First, we employ a
deliberately simple z-score methodology with only two hyperparameters (trend smoothing
length and z-score smoothing length), contrasting with more complex regime-switching
specifications that risk overfitting. This parsimony is motivated by the observation that
factor momentum persists over long horizons, suggesting that simple trend-following rules
should capture the relevant dynamics without requiring precise timing.

Second, we explicitly test hyperparameter stability across sub-periods (1997-2011 and
2011-2025) rather than relying solely on in-sample optimization. This approach addresses
the concerns raised by Harvey et al. [2016] and McLean and Pontiff [2016] regarding data
mining and post-publication decay. Our stability analysis reveals that the chosen hyper-

                                            3
parameters represent a robust compromise between signal responsiveness and transaction
cost mitigation.

Third, we validate practical implementability using tradeable ETFs, addressing the gap
between theoretical factor premia and realizable returns identified by Glushkov [2016].
Our ETF-based backtest incorporates realistic transaction costs, execution lags, and ex-
pense ratios, providing a more accurate assessment of achievable performance.

Fourth, unlike many regime-switching applications that estimate separate parameters
for each factor, we impose homogeneous hyperparameters across all factors. This con-
straint sacrifices some in-sample fit but enhances out-of-sample robustness by reducing
the effective degrees of freedom.

Finally, we provide a comprehensive statistical validation of regime identification using
lagged signals to avoid look-ahead bias, finding statistically significant differentiation
between Bull and Bear regimes across all five factors examined. This rigorous validation
addresses the concerns about regime identification uncertainty raised by Nystrup et al.
[2017].


3    Motivation
We observe that active equity factor returns tend to exhibit momentum persistence over
medium to long-term horizons. We see this visually from Figure 1 of cumulative active
returns of various US equity factors over time.




                Figure 1: Cumulative Active Returns of Equity Factors



                                            4
Given that factor momentum persistence appears to be present at the investment horizon
of months to years, rather than days to weeks, we hypothesize that a simple momentum
approach could be sufficient to capture the regime dynamics of these factors.

Our signal design philosophy is to keep things as simple as possible, in order to maximize
interpretability and minimize the risk of overfitting. As a result, we opt for a straightfor-
ward z-score approach, that measures the deviation of momentum away from long term
trends. This approach has only 2 hyperparameters to tune (the smoothing lengths for
trend estimation and z-score smoothing), which makes it feasible to perform a grid search
over the hyperparameter space, and also makes it easier to interpret the results. We dive
into the details of our methodology in the next section.


4     Methodology
4.1    Data
Our analysis utilizes the dataset of tradeable US equity factors derived from MSCI indices.
For the tradeable factor ETFs, we use their underlying benchmark indices as a proxy for
the factor exposures in our analysis, given the longer history of data available, which
spans back to the 1990s.

Our momentum analysis relies on defining regimes around active factor returns, rather
than total factor returns. We do this in order to isolate the effects of market beta, and
try to capture the pure factor momentum effect. We estimate the betas of each factor
against market (S&P 500) and find that the betas are all close to 1, and reconstruct a
time series of active factor returns based on the beta = 1 assumption; i.e., our active
factor returns time series is the cumulative returns of factor returns less market returns.

4.2    Signal Generation
The core of the strategy is the identification of “Bull” and “Bear” regimes for each factor.
The signal generation process involves the following steps:

     halflife of 60 days.

     and standard deviation:

As mentioned before, our signal design choice is based on choosing the simplest model
with the fewest number of hyperparameters. As such, we’ve decided to stick with a simple
z-score model with smoothing, given our hypothesis that factor returns should exhibit
very long momentum persistence, and therefore if our hypothesis is correct, then a simple
model with few hyperparameters should be sufficient to capture the appropriate regime
dynamics, even if the timing may not be perfect.

In order to further reduce the number of hyperparameters in our model, we use an
expanding window for the calculation of z-scores rather than using a rolling window,
which would have introduced an additional model hyperparameter (i.e., lookback window
length).

We note that active factor returns are noisy, which is why our signal design starts with
an initial smoothing step over a lookback window. Then, we normalize these smoothed
returns to a z-score to get a sense of how significant the trend is relative to historical
trends. Finally, we smooth the z-scores again over a lookback window in order to avoid
whipsaws in regime identification.

Using this methodology, we have 2 hyperparameters to tune, and for intuition sake, we
set both to 60 trading days, or roughly 3 months, which seems like a reasonable time
period to capture medium-term trends. Finally, in order to define the regimes, we set a
simple threshold at 0, where we define a bear regime as one where the z-score is negative,
implying that returns are trending below historical levels, and vice versa for a bull regime.
We are aware that this naive regime assignment approach will probably be slow to react
to turning points of momentum, but we posit that it may not detrimental to performance

                                             6
given momentum regime persistence, and we also gain interpretability of the model.

To prevent overfitting, we use the same hyperparameters across all factors, rather than
optimizing for each one. Given our observation that factor momentum tends to persist
over long time horizons, we believe that this approach is sufficient to capture the regime
dynamics without introducing excessive complexity.

This asymmetry between Bull and Bear regimes is actually advantageous for our long-only
portfolio construction approach—we can confidently overweight factors in Bull regimes
knowing they exhibit strong cross-sectional consistency, while the weaker Bear regime
signal suggests a more conservative approach to underweighting may be appropriate.


5.2    Parameter Stability
A question arises around the stability of the hyperparameters over time, and importantly,
the selection of the hyperparameters themselves. In our case, we have 2 main hyper-
parameters, which are the smoothing lengths for the trend estimation and the z-score
smoothing. Both of these are currently set to 60 days in our analysis.

In order to get a sense of how stable our selection of hyperparameters are, we run the
hyperparameters over a 2x2 grid of values, and for each combination of hyperparameters,
we measure the dispersion between the ann. returns of the Bull and Bear regimes, where
positive values indicate that Bull regimes outperform Bear regimes, while negative values
indicate the opposite. Our aim is to find the hyperparameter combinations that yields
the highest positive dispersion between Bull and Bear regimes.

We also split our data into 2 sub-periods (1997-2011 and 2011-2025) in order to assess
the stability of the hyperparameter performance across time.

Figure 4 shows the average robustness across all factors.

We notice that on average across the factors, the hyperparameter combination that yields
the highest positive dispersion tends towards the top left of the grid, which corresponds to
shorter smoothing lengths for both trend estimation and z-score smoothing. This suggests
that more responsive signals tend to perform better in capturing momentum dynamics.
Conversely, on the bottom right of the grid, we actually see a tendency towards negative
dispersion, which implies that at long window lengths, factors tend to be more mean-
reverting rather than momentum-driven.

Given that we want to capture momentum dynamics, we want to choose a hyperparameter
combination in the top left of the hyperparameter grid. However, choosing the shortest
windows where the positive dispersion is highest also increases the frequency of regime
shifts, which leads to higher transaction costs in a live trading scenario.

Therefore, our initial selection of 60 days for both hyperparameters appears to be a
reasonable compromise, as it still yields positive dispersion across most factors, while
avoiding excessive sensitivity to short-term noise.

We also note that the hit rate in Bull regimes is still consistently above 50% across most
hyperparameter combinations, as seen in Figure 5.

5.3    Portfolio Construction and Backtest
Our factor regime forecasts are based on active factor returns, but when we construct our
portfolio, we use the long-only factor exposures instead. Given that each of the factors
has a long-term beta=1 to market, as long as we remain exposed to any combination of


                                            11
the factors, we are able to maintain neutral market exposure, which is necessary for an
all-equity portfolio. Our deviations away from portfolio beta=1 are driven by our factor
regime model that tilts us towards the factors that exhibit positive momentum.

Given our factor regime definitions, we construct a dynamic allocation portfolio that
overweights a particular factor if it is in a Bull regime, and underweights it when it is
in a Bear regime. In theory, we could use the historical regime expected returns as our
input into our optimization, but given the noisiness of factor returns, we opt to define
active views of +5% annualized return in Bull regimes and -5% annualized return in Bear
regimes. This helps stabilize the optimization process and avoid overfitting to historical
data, which may be poor forecasts, albeit directionally correct.

Our optimization process contains regularization components to ensure that the portfolio
remains diversified and remains relatively anchored to an equal-weighted factor portfo-
lio. For example, we impose a tracking error constraint versus an equal-weighted factor
portfolio, maximum weight constraints per factor (capped at 50%), etc. We re-run the
portfolio optimization whenever there is a regime change in any of the factors.

6    Conclusion
This research presents a systematic approach to dynamic factor allocation using momentum-
based regime switching that demonstrates measurable improvements in risk-adjusted re-
turns. Our simple yet robust methodology identifies Bull and Bear regimes across five
equity factors (Value, Size, Momentum, Quality, Growth) using a straightforward z-score
approach with only two hyperparameters.

Our empirical analysis reveals that the regime identification methodology proves sta-
tistically significant across all factors, with Size and Growth showing particularly strong
significance (p < 0.01) and Value, Momentum, and Quality showing moderate significance
(p < 0.05). The Bull regime hit rates consistently exceed 50% across all factors, provid-
ing a foundation for factor timing decisions. Critically, cross-sectional validation con-
firms that Bull regimes exhibit systematic positive forward returns (t = 1.98, p = 0.047)
when aggregated across factors, while Bear regimes show no significant cross-sectional
pattern—an asymmetry that supports our long-only portfolio construction approach of


                                            15
confidently overweighting Bull regimes while taking a more conservative stance on Bear
regime underweighting.

The factor timing strategy delivers meaningful performance improvements over the equal-
weighted benchmark. Using our chosen 60/60 day hyperparameters, the strategy achieves
a 13.0% annualized return versus 11.3% for the benchmark, while maintaining similar
volatility (19.5% vs 19.2%). This translates to a Sharpe ratio improvement of 0.07 (0.66
vs 0.59), with the strategy demonstrating consistent outperformance rather than sporadic
bursts—evidenced by a 78.6% calendar year outperformance hit rate since 1998.

Our hyperparameter stability analysis confirms that shorter smoothing windows (20/20
days) theoretically offer higher regime discrimination but come at a significant cost.
The alternative hyperparameters generate 3x higher turnover (10.86 vs 3.12 annually)
while delivering inferior risk-adjusted returns, validating our conservative hyperparameter
selection as a reasonable compromise between signal responsiveness and transaction costs.

The strategy’s practical viability is demonstrated through ETF-based backtesting, where
performance remains broadly similar to the theoretical model with only modest degrada-
tion due to tracking error and expense ratios (approximately 15-20bps annually). This
suggests the approach is implementable using readily available factor ETFs, with the
optimization framework successfully balancing active views with risk control through
tracking error constraints and position limits.


6.1    Limitations
Several limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, our methodology relies on expanding
window calculations for z-score normalization, which may be slower to adapt to struc-
tural regime changes compared to rolling window approaches. While this design choice
reduces overfitting risk, it implicitly assumes stationarity in the underlying factor return
distributions.

Second, the binary Bull/Bear classification may oversimplify more nuanced market con-
ditions. Periods of sideways factor performance, as observed during 2021, lead to regime
misidentification and consequent underperformance. A multi-state regime model or con-
tinuous regime probability approach might better capture these transitional periods.

Third, our analysis focuses exclusively on U.S. equity factors. While academic litera-
ture suggests factor momentum persists internationally [Asness et al., 2013, Ehsani and
Linnainmaa, 2022], the transferability of our specific hyperparameter choices to non-U.S.
markets remains untested. Cross-border differences in market microstructure, factor def-
initions, and data availability may affect out-of-sample performance.

Fourth, the backtesting period of 1998-2025 encompasses specific market regimes—the

                                            16
dot-com bubble, the 2008 financial crisis, the post-COVID recovery, and the AI-driven
growth rally—that may not be representative of future conditions. The particularly
strong outperformance in 2020 and 2024, driven by momentum in growth and technology
factors, raises questions about performance sustainability in more factor-neutral environ-
ments.

Finally, while we incorporate transaction costs, our analysis does not fully account for
market impact, slippage, or capacity constraints that would affect larger allocations.
The factor ETFs used in our implementation have grown substantially in assets under
management, potentially affecting their ability to deliver pure factor exposure at scale.


6.2    Future Research
Several extensions could enhance this framework. Multi-regime models incorporating
neutral or transitional states may improve regime identification during sideways periods.
Integration of macroeconomic indicators—such as credit spreads, yield curve slope, or
economic surprise indices—could provide complementary information for factor timing.
Extension to international markets would test the robustness of factor momentum globally
while potentially offering diversification benefits.

Beyond traditional factors, the methodology could incorporate sector rotation, regional
tilts, or thematic exposures, creating a more comprehensive tactical allocation framework.
Machine learning approaches, with appropriate regularization to avoid overfitting, might
capture non-linear patterns in factor momentum that linear z-score methods miss.

Finally, investigation of regime-dependent risk management—adjusting volatility targets
or drawdown constraints based on regime uncertainty—could improve risk-adjusted per-
formance during periods of elevated regime identification error.


6.3    Concluding Remarks
By maintaining simplicity in signal generation while employing sophisticated portfolio
optimization techniques, the strategy achieves meaningful risk-adjusted outperformance
with reasonable turnover levels. The consistent nature of the outperformance, combined
with the strategy’s implementability using liquid ETFs, validates the hypothesis that
factor momentum exhibits sufficient persistence to be captured by simple trend-following
signals, providing a foundation for systematic factor timing within the constraints of
real-world portfolio management.

We note that while the absolute improvement in Sharpe ratio (0.07, from 0.59 to 0.66)
may appear modest, the primary contribution of this research lies in establishing the
statistical significance of factor regime dynamics. The Bull and Bear regimes identified


                                           17
by our methodology exhibit statistically significant differences in forward returns across
all five factors examined (p < 0.05), with the cross-sectional validation further confirming
systematic patterns (t = 1.98, p = 0.047 for Bull regimes). This statistical foundation
suggests that the regime identification methodology captures genuine economic phenom-
ena rather than noise.

Given this robust statistical basis, practitioners may experiment with alternative portfolio
construction approaches to more fully exploit these regime dynamics. Potential avenues
include: (i) more aggressive position sizing based on regime conviction, (ii) incorporat-
ing regime signals into risk budgeting frameworks, (iii) combining factor regime signals
with complementary alpha sources such as sector or macro timing, or (iv) employing ma-
chine learning techniques to optimize the translation of regime probabilities into portfolio
weights. The modest Sharpe improvement in our baseline implementation reflects our
deliberate choice of conservative position limits and tracking error constraints—choices
that prioritize robustness over aggressive signal exploitation but that practitioners with
different risk tolerances may wish to revisit.
